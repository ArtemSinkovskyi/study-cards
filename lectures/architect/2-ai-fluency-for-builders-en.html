1. Who is a builder and what is AI Fluency

  • Builders are product people, designers and makers. They own the full arc from customer problem to shipped solution.
  • AI Fluency = working with AI effectively, efficiently, ethically and safely.
  • Course roadmap: 4D Framework, AI capabilities and limitations, Delegation and the Builder's Toolkit, Discernment for code and UX, Shipping and next steps.
  • Delegating implementation is usually safe. Delegating judgment is usually not.
  • Most AI failures trace back to description, discernment or diligence failures made earlier.
  • Builder brief: a reusable context document you paste at the start of any AI session. It holds what you build and for whom, your role, your constraints (team size, stack, timelines, non-negotiables) and where you want AI involved and where you do not. Think of it as onboarding an AI colleague.

2. The 4D Framework: two loops

таблицю можна гортати вбік →
D Meaning Loop
Delegation Deciding what AI handles and what stays with you outer
Description Saying clearly what you need and how AI should approach it inner
Discernment Evaluating what AI gives back before acting on it inner
Diligence Owning the final result fully, regardless of AI's role outer
  • Inner loop (Description + Discernment) guides daily AI interactions: ask, check, refine.
  • Outer loop (Delegation + Diligence) guides bigger decisions: when and how to use AI at all, and who is accountable.
  • The 4Ds are dynamic, not a sequence: fluency means moving between them while you build.

3. What is generative AI (builder view)

  • Generative AI creates new content rather than analyzing existing data.
  • Three developments made modern LLMs possible: the transformer architecture, vast training data, massive compute.
  • Two training stages: pre-training (learn patterns from billions of examples) and fine-tuning (follow instructions helpfully). See Topic 1.
  • Strengths: versatility across tasks, conversational fluency, tool use.
  • Limits: knowledge cutoffs, hallucinations, unreliable complex reasoning.
  • Best results pair your judgment, creativity and oversight with AI's speed and scale.
  • Self-test on a domain you know cold: (1) versatility: explain one topic for a PM, a mid-level engineer and a senior reviewer; did depth really change? (2) hallucination: ask for libraries or docs and check that they exist and the API is current; (3) cutoff + reasoning: ask about a recent change; does it warn that its data may be stale?

4. Delegation and the Builder's Toolkit

Before code, you make a dozen decisions. Delegation is not "should I use AI here?" but "I have a customer problem: how do I break it down and what role does AI play?"

The toolkit has six capabilities, in this order:

таблицю можна гортати вбік →
# Capability AI's role
1 Empathy (who are you building for) weak: AI can surface data and personas, it cannot feel the gap
2 Design helps (my reading)
3 Architecture helps (my reading)
4 Implementation strongest
5 Judgment keep with you
6 Shipping keep with you

AI is strongest in the middle. Delegate implementation freely; keep empathy, judgment and shipping in your hands.

Three collaboration modes per capability:

Mode Who does what
Automation AI does it, you check
Augmentation you and AI work it together
Agency AI operates with latitude inside boundaries you set

Acceptance tests define done before any code exists: 5 to 7 concrete statements a stranger can mark pass or fail. Good: "A patient with a basic smartphone can find the current wait time in under 30 seconds without creating an account." Bad: "The system is easy to use." As AI accelerates implementation, your value shifts to framing problems and raising the bar.

5. Description: the Description Chain

user voice → requirement → technical spec → AI instruction → tests

  • Prompt engineering is only one link of the chain.
  • The builder is the translator at every step. AI cannot hear what the user did not say.
  • Every step needs judgment calls the previous phase did not make for you.
  • Code works but the product does not = a description failure. Find which upstream link broke.
  • Tests are the most precise form of description. A passing test with an unhappy user means you described the wrong intent.
  • A requirement must be measurable: every adjective is a decision ("fast" means how fast).

6. Discernment for code: five lenses

When AI can build a working product in minutes, "working" stops being the bar. Lens 1 is easy to test; by lens 5 you make judgment calls AI cannot.

таблицю можна гортати вбік →
Lens Name Question
1 Functional Integrity Does it work? Correct output for real inputs, not just test data
2 Production Readiness Will it survive production?
3 Problem Fit Is it the right thing for the problem?
4 Experience Quality How does it feel to use?
5 Responsible Impact Who could it harm or exclude?
  • Note: the course spells out only lens 1 in detail; my questions for lenses 2-5 are a short reading of the lens names.
  • Common failure at lens 1: code passes unit tests but breaks on real data the prompt never covered.
  • AI has predictable blind spots: concurrency (including race conditions), caching, security, anything that only breaks at scale. They show up in production, not in development.
  • Taste is a builder skill. AI delivers functional; making it worth using is on you.

7. Discernment for user experience

  • When implementation is fast, experience is the differentiator.
  • "Make it look good" is a wish, not a spec. Describe experience with the precision of a function.
  • Four UX principles: Clarity (every element shows its purpose), Hierarchy (most important first), Accessibility (contrast, screen reader, keyboard), Feedback (acknowledge actions, explain errors).
  • AI does not get accessibility right by default. Specify it, then audit.
  • A good critique and an actionable AI description are different artifacts; learn to translate between them.

8. Diligence: sharing your work

  • You own the outcome, not the output. "AI wrote it" explains nothing and excuses nothing.
  • Shipping has its own vocabulary: migrations, versioning, rate limits, feature flags. AI will not raise them unless asked.
  • Tests make post-launch iteration safe (test-first habit).
  • Prototype freely, ship selectively; be willing to deprecate your own work.
  • Access is a design decision: check who your assumptions exclude.
  • Pre-ship checklist: Understanding (can you explain what the code does?), Testing (edge cases: closed clinic, no data, zero wait), Access, Responsibility (misuse, honesty about AI's role), Feedback loop (how will you know it works after launch?).

9. Looking forward

  • Robust description follows the full chain; a failure at any link cascades downstream.
  • AI output often passes "Does it work?" but stumbles on "Is it the right thing?"
  • Apply all four Ds to one real task this week: which stages to delegate, the full description chain, five lenses, and "would I stand behind it?".

11. Practice questions from the module quiz

1. True or false: AI has predictable blind spots like concurrency, caching and race conditions. True. AI can write code that looks right and passes basic tests but hides bugs in concurrent execution, stale or wrongly invalidated caches, and race conditions (the result depends on the order of operations). Check these areas with extra care, together with thread safety and distributed-systems logic.

2. What is the correct order of the Description Chain? User voice → product requirement → technical spec → AI instruction. It moves from human intent to growing technical detail: what does the user need, what should the product deliver, how will the system do it, what exactly should the AI do. Do not jump from a vague request straight to a prompt.

3. "The code works, but no one wants to use it" is a failure at which lens? Experience Quality. It functions, but users find it confusing or frustrating. (Not Functional Integrity: it works. Not Production Readiness: it does not break in production. Not Responsible Impact: nobody is harmed.)

4. Before shipping something AI helped build, what does Diligence require? Understanding what the code does and being accountable for the outcome. Wrong answers: asking AI to review itself once more, checking that the latest model was used, only checking that AI-generated tests pass.

5. Why write acceptance tests before any code? To give you and AI a shared definition of "done" from the start. They fix expected behavior and success criteria before implementation. Wrong answers: limiting how much AI may change, better syntax, compliance with standard practice.
