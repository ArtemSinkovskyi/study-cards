Topic 4 of 11: Choosing the Right Claude Model (English)

Claude Certified Architect path. Topic 4: the four models, where to start, and when to change model or effort. Unknown words are in the cards: English words. Читати українською · Previous: Topic 3 Source: Anthropic, "Choosing a Claude model for knowledge workers" (7 min). Plans, defaults and limits change; verify in the product.

Checked against the official docs in section 8. The docs themselves say: if unsure, start with Opus. Two frames, do not mix them. Topic 3 teaches the three-tier frame (Haiku, Sonnet, Opus) that the certification pins, and treats Sonnet as the usual start. This topic describes the four-model lineup (Fable, Opus, Sonnet, Haiku) and recommends starting with Opus. Use the three-tier frame for exam questions about tiers; use this topic for real day-to-day choices.

1. What a model choice means

  • Claude comes in four models: Fable, Opus, Sonnet, Haiku. They differ in capability, speed and how much of your usage limit they consume.
  • The model sets how much capability you have: the knowledge, reasoning and judgment Claude brings. Context and steering help, but the model's base knowledge and abilities are fixed.
  • A more capable model is better at ambiguity and at long tasks where one wrong step spoils everything after it. It is more likely to reach the right answer, often in fewer turns.
  • A less capable model answers faster and does its best work on specific, bounded tasks.
  • For most knowledge work, start with Opus. Then adjust two settings: model and effort.
  • Access depends on the plan: Free has Haiku and Sonnet; paid plans add Opus and more headroom; Fable depends on the plan (on some it runs on usage credits). Your admin can also limit which models appear in your menu.

3. When to adjust model or effort

Where to start. The course says Opus is the default on Pro, Max, Ultra and Team (not confirmed in the Help Center, see section 8). Some plans or organizations start you on Sonnet; then start there, same approach: raise effort first, then switch to a more capable model if it still falls short.

What you can adjust (both in the model menu next to the send button):

  • Model: how capable. Switch up (Opus, Fable) or down (Sonnet).
  • Effort: how thorough (levels: Low, Medium, High, Extra high, Max; availability varies by model). Higher: Claude thinks longer, makes more tool calls, checks more of its work. Lower: a quicker, more direct pass.

How to adjust. Start at the default. If the result is wrong, first check that your prompt and files gave Claude what it needed. Then change one setting at a time, effort first, so you can tell what made the difference.

You notice Do this
Instructions get missed, or long work wraps up before it is finished Raise effort
Claude has all the context, effort is raised, and quality is still short Switch to a larger model
Responses get more verbose without better quality, or scope grows beyond what you asked Lower effort
Quality holds after lowering effort and you still want faster answers Try a less capable model (compare results before you rely on it)

4. How settings affect usage

  • Usage is counted in tokens (small units of text): reading your prompt, processing files, thinking and writing all draw on your limit.
  • Model sets what each token costs: more capable generally means a higher cost per token.
  • Effort sets how many tokens a task uses: higher effort means more thorough answers but slower and faster limit use.
  • What matters more is cost per task. A more capable model often finishes a hard task in fewer turns, so it can cost less per task on long or ambiguous work even though each token costs more. Illustration: a clearly defined task takes 1 turn on either model (the less capable one costs less); a long or ambiguous task takes about 6 turns on the less capable model but 2 on the more capable one (which costs less). This is why the course recommends starting on Opus.
  • To use less of your limit, lower effort first. Switching to a less capable model changes both cost and capability; lowering effort changes cost while keeping the model's judgment and reasoning.

5. What it looks like in practice

  • Choose the model that fits how you usually work. If Opus easily handles everything, Sonnet may also fit. If your hardest projects still fall short after raising effort on Opus, try Fable and compare.
  • Two quick questions about the work: how long would it take you without AI? and how clear is the correct output? Longer and less clear work is where a more capable model helps most. Other factors also matter: the stakes, how easily you can verify it, how fast you need it.
  • Rough examples from the course explorer: contract redlining, full reports, sheets and decks, complex analysis suit the stronger models; a short, well-defined task suits Sonnet or Haiku. This is an illustration; the best test is to try it and check whether Claude followed instructions, finished the work and reached the quality you need.

Try it on your own work. Pick two tasks, one bounded and one long multi-step. Run both on Opus and on Sonnet:

  1. Decide what a good result looks like before you run anything.
  2. Same prompt and files, new chat, default effort.
  3. Count how many follow-up turns or retries you needed to get a result you would use.
  4. Compare one task at a time. About the same turns: either model works, keep the one you use. Fewer turns or a finding that matters: use that model for this kind of work.

6. Choosing models over time

  • Each new model changes which tasks are worth handing to Claude; retry more ambitious tasks that hit a ceiling before.
  • With every new model (including a newer version of your current one), revisit your tasks: maybe the more capable model gets the same result at lower effort, which can make it cheaper. Spend a few minutes running your go-to tasks across models.
